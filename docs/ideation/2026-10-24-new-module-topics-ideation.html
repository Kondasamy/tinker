Grounding Context

Codebase Context

  • What exists. There are 17 live modules, all on AI, and the roadmap is empty: new modules start as reader requests (AGENTS.md). The audience is PMs, engineers and analysts at companies adopting AI. They are assumed to have no background, and the maths is optional.
  • Hard constraints. The site is static, with no backend, no API key and no live LLM. Every interactive model output is either recorded for each control state (with coverage tests) or produced by a tiny real model running in the browser. Larkspur mini has 51k parameters, 2 layers and d_model 32, and its weights are capped at 150 KB. "d_model 64 breaks the payload budget: stop and ask."
  • Reusable assets. Larkspur mini's real, deterministic training run, with a committed loss curve that ends at train 3.26 against held-out 4.04. A full fine-tune, LoRA and a distilled student. Evals' code graders (keyFactOf, hasCitation). Multimodal's canvas rasteriser. The recorded-turn engines.
  • Past exclusions. Fine-tuning: "no RLHF/RL chapter (DPO gets one term at most)". Transformers: no MoE chapter. The roadmap ruled out compaction, harness engineering, A2A and agentic commerce. These rulings scoped single modules or that roadmap; none of them bans a topic site-wide.
  • Platform identity: now settled as "how tech works". The site headline already says "See how tech works", but the home description (src/lib/pages.ts:26) still says "interactive explanations of how AI works". After this report was first written, the author chose to include classic tech too, so that description changes when the first classic module goes live.
  • Classic tech fits the build model better than AI does. No model is needed, so every number can come from a live simulator running in the browser, like Inference's server and Context's cache. Larkspur Supply Co. is a hardware company with warehouses, orders, a web shop and card payments, so most classic topics map straight onto its business. src/lib/rag/bm25.ts already builds an inverted index (postings) with Okapi BM25.

External context

  • In 2026, post-training (SFT, then DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards and GRPO) is the leading trend. There is no playable explainer of it at PM level; existing material is written guides and static diagrams.
  • Image diffusion has Polo Club's Diffusion Explainer, which is aimed at students and has no story. Diffusion LLMs (Inception's Mercury 2, launched Feb 2026, with ~1,000 tok/s claimed by the vendor; Gemini Diffusion; LLaDA) have no interactive explainer.
  • The interpretability tools that exist (Anthropic's attribution graphs, Neuronpedia) are aimed at researchers. There is a gap at PM level.
  • For mixture of experts, world models, synthetic data, computer use and coding agents, the search found no interactive explainers. That search was shallow.

1. Post-training: the auto-marked exam

Description
This module shows how a base model becomes an assistant and then a reasoning model, in three steps:
  • SFT. The model learns by imitation. Larkspur mini's sign-off fine-tune already does this.
  • DPO. The reader picks the better of two replies.
  • RLVR / GRPO. The reader writes Larkspur's grader from Evals' code graders. A group of drafts is graded on a curve (each scored against the group's average), and the policy shifts toward the drafts that scored above it.
The final chapter is about reward hacking: a weak grader gets gamed, for example by a reply that signs off perfectly and is wrong. The workbench asks for the smallest grader that can't be gamed. Rollouts are recorded for each grader setting, the same pattern Guardrails uses.
Basis
  • direct: the four-roadmap plan, line 124: "Fine-tuning: no RLHF/RL chapter (DPO gets one term at most)". data/fine-tuning/facts.json:8 holds the site's only DPO content. Verified.
  • direct: src/lib/evals/graders.ts exports keyFactOf (line 30) and hasCitation (line 33). These are a ready-made verifier. Verified.
  • external: post-training is the dominant trend in 2026, and the web search found no playable explainer of it for PMs.
  • All five lenses produced this idea independently.
Rationale
It is the biggest trend with the thinnest interactive coverage. It closes the deliberate hole between Fine-tuning (module 9) and Reasoning (module 11): Reasoning teaches thinking as a setting but never how it was trained. "Your model learned to pass your test, not to be right" is a decision PMs actually face.
Downsides
Evals already teaches code graders, so the module needs its own hook (grading on a curve, and reward hacking) or the first chapter repeats Evals. Real GRPO checkpoints would mean new Python training. Recorded rollouts are the safe path, and the copy must say so.

2. Pre-training: watch a model grow

Description
The site ships checkpoints from Larkspur mini's real training run. The reader scrubs through training time and types the same prompt at each checkpoint. They watch it go from noise to word pieces to words to Larkspur facts, and then see training loss and held-out loss split apart as the model starts memorising. Later chapters cover the data mix and deduplication (a document repeated ten times turns the model into a parrot). They also cover contamination: leak the test questions into training and the score jumps while held-out quality stays flat. If a spike shows a 51k model can collapse visibly, there is an optional model collapse chapter, which trains generation N+1 on generation N's text.
Basis
  • direct: the lossCurve in public/data/lm/meta.json: step 100 train 4.98 / held-out 4.96; step 3000 train 3.26 / held-out 4.04. Verified.
  • direct: scripts/train/README.md:19: "a retrain gives a byte-identical model.bin". Checkpoints are therefore just extra saves.
  • external: AnimatedLLM (2026) animates pre-training, but nobody lets you hold the real model at step 100 and at step 3000.
Rationale
Tokens, Transformers and Fine-tuning all quote this training run, but none of them teaches it. Extending "nothing is faked" to training itself is the strongest form of Tinker's promise. The contamination chapter brings in the most useful part of the "benchmarks you can't trust" idea.
Downsides
Ten checkpoints at about 100 KB each is roughly 1 MB, over the 800 KB voice precedent. That needs a budget decision, or fewer checkpoints or Float16 deltas. The checkpoints must stay separate from model.bin, so the fine-tunes, the student and the goldens don't change. The model-collapse chapter is unproven at this size.

3. Interpretability: pull the fuse

Description
Interpretability works by causing changes and watching what happens. The reader switches off heads and layers of the real Larkspur mini (like an electrician pulling fuses to find which circuit runs the porch light) and watches the top 5 next tokens change. They hunt down the word-start head that is already measured. They use a "logit lens" to watch the guess form layer by layer. Then they push the model along a direction (steering) and watch it drift toward talking about drills. Everything runs live; nothing is recorded.
Basis
  • direct: src/lib/lm/model.ts:31-40 ForwardResult returns ids, truncated, logits and attention, with no residual-stream or ablation hooks. Those hooks are a small, contained addition. Verified.
  • direct: AGENTS.md: the word-start head is layer 0, head 0, scoring 0.39 against 0.09 for even spread, and a test recomputes it.
  • external: attribution graphs and Neuronpedia are researcher-facing, so a PM-level explainer is a gap.
Rationale
This is the one trend where Tinker's tiny model is an advantage rather than a toy: the reader can open the whole model in a browser. It answers "can anyone see why it said that?" with an experiment rather than a slogan. It is also the cheapest build on this list, because no new model and no recordings are needed.
Downsides
In a model with 2 layers and 2 heads, ablation may change too little to read, or heads may duplicate each other. Check how much a lesion actually moves the output before committing to chapters. Transformers' heads chapter is next door, so the edge here has to be cause rather than description.

4. Diffusion: noise to picture

Description
Every generator on the site today types left to right. Diffusion edits instead: it starts from noise and removes its best guess of the noise at each step. Larkspur needs product pictures. The reader scrubs denoising steps on Larkspur part drawings, changes the step count, the guidance (how hard the prompt steers each step) and the seed, and sees why more steps cost money and why hands and text fail. One buildable route is a real toy diffusion on 2-D points, a tiny network small enough to run live, beside recorded image trajectories of the iso engine's part drawings.
Basis
  • direct: the repo has no code for diffusion, denoising or noise schedules. src/content/multimodal/kit.tsx:76-88 already has a pixel-faithful canvas preview, and src/lib/multimodal/images.ts draws parts as deterministic SVG, so the training images are licence-clean.
  • external: Polo Club's Diffusion Explainer is aimed at students, with no story or decision. You named this topic.
Rationale
It is the generation family the site has never shown, and the most visual module we could build. "Editing, not typing" also prepares the ground for idea 5.
Downsides
The verifier refuted a learned 16×16 image denoiser under about 150 KB: new tooling, and quality probably too poor to teach from. So the image half must be recorded trajectories, or a hand-coded schedule, with the copy saying so. The 2-D toy needs a feasibility spike. This is the highest build risk of the survivors.

5. Diffusion LLMs: writing in any order

Description
Larkspur mini writes a reply left to right. A diffusion language model starts with every slot masked and fills them like a crossword: the confident squares first, then the rest, revising as it goes. The reader sets how many tokens are revealed per pass and sees the trade between speed and coherence. They also see why structured text like JSON suits the approach. They leave able to judge a "1,000 tok/s" claim by naming what it gives up.
Basis
  • external: Mercury 2 (Feb 2026, speed claimed by the vendor), Gemini Diffusion and LLaDA exist, and no interactive explainer was found.
  • direct: Inference already teaches decoding as the bottleneck and speculative decoding as parallel drafting. Structured outputs already has a mask that limits which tokens may fill a slot, so that idea carries over.
Rationale
It is the newest of these trends, and PMs will hear speed claims they can't evaluate. It is a different concept from image diffusion, so it is a module of its own under the "one concept per module" rule.
Downsides
Larkspur mini only reads leftward (causal), so it can't really fill in parallel. The unmasking orders would be recorded, or would need a new bidirectional model under the payload ceiling. Vendor speed figures must be framed as the vendor's, with a date.

6. Computer use: the agent that clicks

Description
Larkspur's old supplier portal has no API. An agent that sees only a screenshot must click coordinates to place an order. The reader sees what the agent sees (Multimodal's patches over a screen drawn in code), predicts where it will click, and meets the failures: a button that moved, a pop-up, a stale screenshot, and a look-alike "Pay now" button. Controls are screenshot size (which sets cost in image tokens), checking the screen after each action, and an approval gate. The workbench asks: build the API integration, or let the agent drive the screen?
Basis
  • direct: the repo has no computer-use content. src/lib/multimodal/patches.ts already counts visual tokens per 28 px patch, and images.ts already draws deterministic SVG screens.
  • direct: the turn pattern Agents uses, recorded for each state and checked by coverage tests, fits a small set of screen states.
Rationale
Teams are adopting this kind of agent now, and its failures are visual, which suits a site that leads with pictures. It joins Agents, Multimodal and Sandboxing in a way none of them does alone.
Downsides
It overlaps Guardrails, since text injected on screen is prompt injection, and Sandboxing. The module's own angle must be grounding and coordinates, not the threat model again. Its prerequisites would be Agents and Multimodal, which gives the prerequisite graph a depth of 4.

7. Calibration: does it know when it's wrong?

Description
When the weather forecaster says 70%, does it rain 70% of the time? The reader builds a real reliability diagram, computed live from Larkspur mini's own probabilities on held-out text, and sees where it is overconfident. They try temperature as a fix and set an "abstain below X" rule. The site's hand-set "confidently wrong" answers become the case file.
Basis
  • direct: src/content/small-models/05-fall-back-when-unsure.tsx:84: "These are stand-ins set by hand; real systems compute them." Verified.
  • direct: evaluate() in model.ts already computes held-out top-1 accuracy, so grouping predictions by stated confidence is a few lines of code.
Rationale
It replaces a fixture the site openly calls a stand-in with numbers computed in the reader's own browser, and "can I trust the score?" is a top PM question.
Downsides
Larkspur mini is overfit (top-5 about 44%), so its calibration is a toy. The topic is narrow for seven chapters and may be better as a chapter in Small models or in idea 3. Hallucination tracing was split off because it overlaps RAG, Evals and Observability.

C1. Selling the last hinge twice: replication and consistency

Description
Larkspur's stock lives in three warehouses, and each one keeps its own copy of the stock count. Two customers buy the last hinge at the same moment, and both orders go through. The reader cuts the link between warehouses (a network partition), chooses to stay open (and risk overselling) or refuse orders (and lose sales), and tries quorum reads and writes, leader election and "eventually consistent". The workbench asks for a setting per product: a cheap hinge can be oversold, a one-off drill press cannot.
Basis
  • reasoned: distributed consistency is the classic topic engineers find hardest and PMs meet as "why did we oversell?" It plays well because the failure is concrete: two receipts for one hinge.
  • direct: the Inference server is a step-by-step simulator in time (AGENTS.md Inference engine), and this can follow the same pattern.
Rationale
It fits Larkspur's business better than any other classic topic, and it turns the CAP trade-off into a business decision for each product.
Downsides
The level is Advanced, so it needs care to stay maths-optional. Sticking to warehouses and stock counts keeps the theory off the page.

C2. Caching and CDNs: Larkspur's warehouses are edge caches

Description
A central depot ships to regional warehouses. That arrangement is a CDN: the depot is the origin, and each warehouse is an edge cache holding copies close to customers. The reader decides what to stock where and sets expiry dates (TTL). A price change leaves old prices sitting at the edges, so the reader has to invalidate those copies. A flash sale then floods the depot with requests all at once (a thundering herd). The last chapter shows that prompt caching (Context) and the KV cache (Inference) are the same idea.
Basis
  • direct: this was rejected earlier only because the platform's identity was unsettled. That is no longer true.
  • direct: Context's cache.ts (hits, lifetimes, expiry) and Inference's simulator are patterns to reuse.
Rationale
It is the most reusable idea in computing, it connects to two live modules, and the metaphor is nearly free.
Downsides
There are many explainers for this already, so the story and the link to the AI modules have to be what sets it apart.

C3. Why is the dashboard slow? Databases and indexes

Description
Larkspur's order table has a million rows, and "orders for customer 1042" takes seconds. The reader watches a full scan read every row, adds an index and watches a B-tree jump straight to the rows it needs, and sees the cost: every new order now has to update the index too. Later chapters cover the query plan, joins, and adding an index on the wrong column. The rows-read counter is computed live.
Basis
  • reasoned: "why is it slow?" is the most common question a PM brings to an engineer, and it lives in the database. The data volumes and orders come straight from the story.
  • direct: src/lib/scale.ts already has a size slider that goes from one document to petabytes, so there is a pattern for "watch it grow".
Rationale
Every reader's company has a database. An index trades faster reads for slower writes, and that trade-off pays off for the rest of their career.
Downsides
B-trees are hard to draw in the isometric picture style. It may need flat figures (flat.ts) rather than a scene.

C4. Did the new button work? A/B testing

Description
Larkspur tests a new checkout button. The reader runs the experiment with a seeded simulator of visitors. They stop it early on a lucky day and declare a fake win (peeking). They test twenty colours and find one that "wins" by chance. They see how sample size and effect size decide how long a test must run. The workbench is a launch decision: ship, keep testing, or stop.
Basis
  • reasoned: PMs run experiments every week and misread them often, so the payoff for this audience is the highest of any idea here.
  • direct: the house sampler is seeded (mulberry32, AGENTS.md Transformers engine), so simulated traffic can be reproduced and tested in e2e.
Rationale
It is the decision-making module the audience lacks, and Evals' "is version B better?" makes a natural sequel.
Downsides
Statistics fights the "maths optional" rule. The pictures (a coin-flip wall, bars that move) have to carry the ideas.

C5. Search before AI: the inverted index

Description
The reader builds an index of Larkspur's documents by hand: each word points to the documents that contain it. They then rank results by term weight (BM25), and finally see where keyword search fails on meaning and where embeddings win, and why an exact part number still beats vectors. It becomes a new Beginner root that leads into RAG.
Basis
  • direct: src/lib/rag/bm25.ts already builds postings (an inverted index) with Okapi BM25 (k1 1.2, b 0.75) over the Larkspur corpus. The engine already exists.
Rationale
It is the cheapest build on the list, and it gives readers who aren't into AI a first step into the RAG path.
Downsides
RAG already shows hybrid search, so this module must stop before embeddings take over. Adding a prerequisite to RAG is fine, because progress is keyed by ids and prerequisites only inform.

C6. What happens when you open a page: DNS, TCP, TLS and HTTP

Description
A customer opens larkspur.example. The reader follows the trip: DNS looks up the address, TCP opens a connection, TLS proves the site's identity and encrypts the line, HTTP fetches the page, and packets are lost and resent. The reader adds distance (latency) and loss and watches each handshake cost round trips. The chapter on certificates and keys covers what HTTPS actually proves.
Basis
  • reasoned: this is the classic computing explainer, and the audience uses it every day without a picture of it. Network trips draw well as flat lanes (flat.ts: lanes, cards, arrows).
Rationale
It is foundational and has a Beginner entry point. Counting round trips explains why "the site is slow from Sydney".
Downsides
This is the most crowded topic: many good explainers already exist. Encryption could be its own module instead of one chapter.

C7. Card payments: where the money actually goes

Description
A customer pays Larkspur by card. The reader follows authorisation, capture, settlement and fees through the card network to the issuing bank, then meets a declined card, a double charge and a chargeback. The workbench sets when to capture (at order or at shipping) for different products.

C8. Git: how history is stored

Description
Larkspur's team edits the price list. The reader sees each commit saved as a snapshot named by its own content hash, a branch shown to be just a pointer to one of them, and a merge that joins two lines of history and sometimes conflicts. Rebase and revert show that history can be rewritten, and what that costs a team.
Basis
  • reasoned: PMs and analysts work with engineers who live in Git, and the model of snapshots and pointers is small, exact, and easy to simulate in the browser.
Rationale
It gives non-engineers a model of the tool their team argues about. The pictures are clean: snapshots on a graph, with pointers as flags.
Downsides
It is crowded (Learn Git Branching exists), and engineers already know it, so the payoff is mostly for PMs and analysts.
